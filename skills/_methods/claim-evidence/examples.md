# Claim-and-evidence examples

These invented examples illustrate reasoning; they are not evidence for real studies.

## Compound claim

Sentence:

> The intervention improved recall and eliminates long-term relapse (Lee,
> 2024).

Inspected source:

> Reports improved immediate recall; does not measure relapse.

Response:

Retain and calibrate the recall clause. Remove, qualify, or find support for the
relapse clause. Correct citation format does not support the unsupported claim.

## Numerical trace

Bad:

> Accuracy increased by 12%.

Better claim map:

- claim: accuracy increased by 12 percentage points;
- support: `outputs/model-3.csv`, contrast row 4;
- scope: analyzed sample after stated exclusions;
- limitation: interval includes effects smaller than the prose implies.

Why:

The map distinguishes percent from percentage points and connects wording to an
inspectable output.

## Citation roles

A methods primer by Hart (2024) explains meta-analysis but does not assess
whether it helps identify sources of variation in field studies.

> Meta-analysis can help identify sources of variation across field studies
> (Hart, 2024).

Here the citation reads as support for the whole claim. If the usefulness
claim is supported elsewhere, keep it and attach the primer to the method:

> Meta-analysis (Hart, 2024) can help identify sources of variation
> across field studies.

If readers may still take Hart as support for the whole sentence, write
“Meta-analysis (for an introduction, see Hart, 2024) …” or cite the primer
where the method is introduced.

A garden survey by Ives (2023) describes irrigation problems at a few
sites. It is relevant to a broader claim about shared gardens without
establishing that claim:

> Shared gardens can be difficult to keep watered (for related cases,
> see Ives, 2023).

If Rao (2025) disputes Ives's explanation for the irrigation failures, name
the disagreement rather than listing the two sources as if they agreed:

> Ives (2023) attributed the irrigation failures to unclear duties, but
> Rao (2025) pointed to limited water access at the same sites.

If the disagreement is incidental to the argument, a short cue can suffice:
“(see Ives, 2023; for a different explanation of those failures, see Rao,
2025).” In each version the broader claim needs its own support if it does
not follow from the evidence already presented.

## Support without certainty

Suppose a library moved hard-to-find books to labelled shelves. In an
observational study, visitors made fewer requests for help finding those books,
though staffing changed at the same time. The evidence supports easier
navigation as an explanation, even if the paper describes only the change in
requests. The staffing change matters because visitors may have had fewer
opportunities to ask. A second outcome, such as whether visitors found the
book they wanted, would help tell these explanations apart.

> Clearer shelf labels may help visitors find books on their own.

The evidence warrants the possibility. Adding that the mechanism remains
unproven would repeat what “may” conveys. The staffing explanation is useful
if the discussion asks how to test the mechanism.
