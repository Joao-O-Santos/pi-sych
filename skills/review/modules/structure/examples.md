# Structure examples

## Structural duplication

Input: a manuscript repeats its contribution in four places but never explains
why it matters.

Output: identify which paragraphs perform the same job, preserve the strongest
statement, and propose one revised argument sequence before suggesting sentence
edits.

## Collaborative and adversarial lenses

Collaborative reading:

> The intended contribution appears to be X, but paragraphs 4–6 delay the
> premise needed to understand it. Moving that premise earlier would let the
> existing argument work with fewer repetitions.

Adversarial reading:

> The manuscript presents X as its contribution, but the text has not yet shown
> that X differs materially from Y. The contribution claim is therefore not
> earned in its current form.

Both can be valid. The difference is posture, not permission to invent findings.

## Project-state divergence

`PROJECT.md` says the paper's central claim is A. The current discussion argues
for B.

A useful review finding is:

> The manuscript now appears to make B its central claim, while `PROJECT.md`
> still records A. This may be an intentional change or project-state drift.
> Confirm which direction is accepted before a structural rewrite or durable
> project-state update.

Do not silently rewrite B back to A or update `PROJECT.md` on the reviewer's own
authority.
